01

Perception · cognitive illusion

Your brain is lying to you.

Three quick tests. In each one, your visual system uses context to make a guess. The ruler disagrees.

Perception test
0 / 3 revealed

Which horizontal line is longer?

Which center circle is larger?

A
B
Both inner squares are exactly #7b8794

Which inner square is lighter?

The idea

Your visual system does not measure pixels. It infers size and lightness from context: edges, neighbours, and expected shape.

That is usually useful. Here, the context is doing the lying.

What is actually happening?

The Müller-Lyer illusion changes apparent length with the direction of the arrowheads. The Ebbinghaus illusion changes apparent size through the size of surrounding circles. Simultaneous contrast changes apparent lightness through the background.

These are not errors in your eyes. They are by-products of a visual system that is tuned to compare and interpret, not to act as a ruler.

Illusion strength varies by person, display, and viewing distance. The measurements are the objective part; the perception is not.

What did you discover?

Your estimate came from context, not a ruler. The lines were identical; the fins changed the guess. Subjective perception is not always a reliable measurement.

Try this next: hide the fins and look again. Does the correction feel immediate, or does the illusion linger?

02

Probability · intuition vs. counting

The coin is not as fair as you think.

Three situations where the structure of the possibilities matters more than the story your gut tells you.

Probability playground

Pick a door. One hides a prize.

Stay wins00%
Switch wins00%
50.7%chance of a shared birthday with 23 people

Which exact 10-flip sequence is more likely?

Now predict the longest run of heads in 100 flips.

The idea

Probability is about the structure of possible outcomes, not just what feels likely. A host who knows where the prize is, or a room full of birthdays, changes what the evidence means.

Why switching wins two-thirds of the time

Your first pick is right with probability 1/3. The host then opens a losing door that you did not pick, and always can. If your first pick was wrong (2/3 of the time), the remaining unopened door must be the prize. Switching wins exactly in those cases.

The host's action is not random. It is constrained by knowledge, and that is where the information comes from.

Birthday calculations assume 365 equally likely birthdays, independence, and no February 29. Real birthdays are not perfectly uniform.

What did you discover?

Switching wins about twice as often as staying. Streaks are expected in random data. And 23 people are enough to make a shared birthday more likely than not.

Try this next: run the Monty Hall simulation again. Does the estimate move closer to 33% and 67%, or does it wobble?

Chaos · sensitivity to initial conditions

A tiny change. A completely different future.

Two double pendulums, the same equations, the same masses and lengths. Only the starting angle of B differs. Watch the futures separate.

Double pendulum · RK4
t = 0.00 s separation = 0.000 m together
A · θ₁trail
B · θ₁ + Δtrail
Quick Δ

Both simulations use the same physics function. Only B's initial θ₁ is offset by Δ. This is an ideal, frictionless model.

The idea

A chaotic system can be deterministic and still practically unpredictable. The equations have one exact future, but tiny measurement errors grow exponentially.

This is sensitivity to initial conditions, not randomness.

What makes it chaotic?

The double pendulum is governed by coupled nonlinear equations. Nearby starting states do not stay nearby: the separation grows until the two motions look unrelated.

Not every complicated system is chaotic, and not every chaotic system is unpredictable at every timescale. The claim is narrower: long-term prediction needs extremely precise measurements.

The simulation uses RK4 at a fixed 0.0025 s timestep. Numerical drift is possible over long runs, especially near high-energy configurations.

What did you discover?

Two trajectories with almost identical starting conditions separated into visibly different paths. The rules did not change; the precision did.

Try this next: set Δ to 0.001° and watch how long the paths stay together. Then set it to 1° and compare.

04

Algorithms · search and strategy

Can you outsmart an algorithm?

Draw walls, trace a route by hand, then watch breadth-first search and A* explore the same maze differently.

Predict first

Which search will explore fewer cells on your maze?

The idea

BFS expands outward in every direction and finds a shortest path when every step costs the same. A* adds a heuristic to bias the search toward the goal.

The heuristic changes where the search looks, not the rules of the maze.

Are the comparisons fair?

Yes: both searches use the same maze, start, destination, and unit step cost. BFS is guaranteed to find a shortest path in an unweighted graph. A* with Manhattan distance is also guaranteed here, because the heuristic never overestimates.

A* often explores fewer cells, but that is not a universal law. A weaker heuristic, a weighted graph, or a different representation can change the comparison.

The timing shown is computation time, not animation time. On a maze this small, both searches are effectively instant.

05

Emergence · local rules, global behavior

The tipping point.

A grid of cells follows one small rule. No conductor, no plan. Play, pause, and change a single cell to see what the future does.

Conway's Game of Life
gen 0 alive 0 density 0%
Patterns

Pulsar preset. Deterministic: the same start gives the same future.

The idea

Conway's Game of Life has four local rules: a live cell with two or three live neighbours survives; a dead cell with exactly three live neighbours becomes alive; everything else changes to dead.

That is all. The motion, oscillation, and decay you see are emergent.

Where is the controller?

There isn't one. Each cell only looks at its eight neighbours. The global pattern is a consequence of many local decisions, not a plan imposed from above.

This bounded grid treats cells outside the edges as dead. On an infinite grid, some patterns continue much longer.

The preset patterns are fixed and deterministic. The random soup uses a seeded generator, so the same seed reproduces the same start.

What did you discover?

Simple local rules produced motion, oscillation, and decay without a central controller. The complexity is in the interactions, not in any single cell.

Try this next: load the pulsar, then toggle one cell. Does the symmetry break immediately, or does it take a few generations?

Numbers · structure and surprise

The world of weird numbers.

A sequence that may climb forever, primes that fall out of a sieve, a ratio that keeps approaching φ, and the same number in a different alphabet.

The idea

Simple rules can reveal surprising structure. The Collatz rule is almost embarrassingly short, yet no one has proved that every starting number eventually reaches 1.

An interesting pattern is not automatically a proof.

What is the Collatz conjecture?

Start with a positive integer. If it is even, halve it. If it is odd, triple it and add one. The conjecture says every starting value eventually reaches 1.

It has been checked for enormous ranges, but a finite check is not a proof for every positive integer. That distinction is the point.

The sieve and Fibonacci identities shown here are proved mathematics. The Collatz behavior you see is a computed example, not a proof of the conjecture.

What did you discover?

A four-word rule can produce a long, wildly climbing sequence. A sieve can reveal primes by elimination. A ratio can approach φ without ever landing on it. And a pattern can be real without being proved.

Try this next: find a number under 100 with more Collatz steps than 27. Then ask whether that proves anything about all numbers.

Lab notes, assumptions, and limitations
  • Monty Hall simulation: the host always knows the prize location and always opens a losing door that the player did not choose. The simulation runs both strategies on the same trials.
  • Birthday paradox: 365 equally likely birthdays, independent people, no February 29. Real birthday distributions are not uniform.
  • Double pendulum: ideal frictionless model, RK4 integration at a fixed 0.0025 s timestep. Long runs can accumulate numerical drift. The two simulations share one physics implementation.
  • Maze: 23 × 13 bounded grid with unit step costs. BFS and A* use the same maze, start, and goal. A* uses Manhattan distance. A* is not guaranteed to explore fewer nodes on every graph or with every heuristic.
  • Game of Life: bounded 44 × 26 grid; cells outside the edges are dead. Presets are deterministic. The random soup is reproducible from its seed.
  • Coin streaks: each flip is independent with probability 1/2. The exact 10-flip sequences in the comparison are equally likely as exact sequences.
  • All computation happens in your browser. No input is sent anywhere.
